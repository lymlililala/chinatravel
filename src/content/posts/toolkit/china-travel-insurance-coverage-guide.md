## Table of contents

Travel insurance for China is not optional — it's essential. The country's medical system operates on a pay-upfront-first basis; international hospitals in major cities can charge ¥3,000–¥10,000 ($420–$1,400) per day; and medical evacuation from remote areas (Sanjiangyuan, Kekexili, the Tibetan Plateau) can cost $30,000–$100,000+ without insurance coverage.

This guide helps you understand what you actually need, avoid common policy gaps and get the most value for your insurance spending.

## What Standard Travel Insurance Covers (and What It Doesn't)

### What Most Standard Policies Include

**Emergency medical expenses:** Hospital treatment, emergency surgery, prescription medicines for acute conditions. This is the core protection you need.

**Medical evacuation and repatriation:** Transport to the nearest appropriate medical facility and, if necessary, repatriation to your home country. Critical for China given the distance from major hospitals in remote areas.

**Trip cancellation/interruption:** Refunds for non-refundable travel costs if you have to cancel before departure or cut your trip short due to covered reasons (illness, family emergency, etc.).

**Baggage and personal effects:** Compensation for lost, stolen or damaged luggage.

**Travel delays:** Compensation for accommodation and meals during significant travel delays.

### What Standard Policies Often Exclude

**Pre-existing conditions:** Many policies exclude any medical claim connected to a condition you had before purchase. If you have diabetes, heart disease, asthma or any chronic condition, specifically check the exclusions.

**High-altitude activities:** Standard policies often exclude altitude-related illness above a specified height (commonly 3,000–4,500m depending on the policy). If you're trekking in Qinghai, Tibet or high Yunnan/Sichuan, check this specifically.

**Adventure activities:** Rock climbing, river rafting, motorcycling, skiing and other adventure activities are often excluded unless a specific adventure activity add-on is purchased.

**Pandemics/epidemics:** COVID-19 coverage varies enormously by policy; check current terms carefully.

**Extreme sports:** Paragliding, bungee jumping and similar activities are excluded from most standard policies.

## What China Specifically Requires

### Medical Cover Minimums for China

A minimum medical coverage of $100,000 USD (approximately ¥700,000) is strongly recommended. Treatments at major international hospitals in Shanghai, Beijing or Guangzhou can reach this level for serious accidents or significant illness.

**Recommended minimum coverage:**
- Emergency medical: $150,000+
- Medical evacuation: $200,000+

For remote area travel (Tibetan Plateau, Qinghai, far western Xinjiang), increase these figures and specifically confirm your policy covers helicopter evacuation.

### Why Medical Evacuation Coverage is Critical

China's geography means that if you're injured or seriously ill in a remote area:
- The nearest hospital may be 4–8 hours' drive away on rough roads
- Helicopter evacuation from the Tibetan Plateau to Chengdu costs approximately $25,000–$50,000
- Medical evacuation from China to your home country ranges from $50,000 to $250,000+

Without insurance, these costs fall entirely on you.

## How the China Medical System Works with Insurance

### Public vs Private Hospitals

**Public hospitals (公立医院):**
- Cheaper treatment costs
- Payment required upfront at reception; insurance processes reimbursement later
- Less English-language capability
- Higher quality at tier-3 hospitals (三甲医院, the top classification)

**International/private hospitals (国际医院, 私立医院):**
- More expensive but handle insurance billing directly (cashless in many cases)
- English-speaking staff
- Located primarily in major cities

### Cashless vs Reimbursement

Some insurers have direct billing arrangements with major Chinese hospitals (particularly in Beijing and Shanghai), allowing cashless treatment. Most, however, operate on a reimbursement basis:
- You pay for treatment upfront
- You document everything (receipts, medical reports, prescriptions)
- You submit a claim after returning home

**For cashless treatment:** Your insurer's emergency assistance line can help identify hospitals in your city that have direct billing agreements.

### The Emergency Assistance Number

Every travel insurance policy has a 24-hour emergency assistance number. Save this number before you travel; it's different from the regular claims number.

In a medical emergency in China, call this number first (before or simultaneously with going to the hospital). The assistance team can:
- Recommend the most appropriate hospital
- Arrange cashless billing where available
- Organise medical evacuation if needed
- Provide translation assistance

## Practical Insurance Tips for China

### Documentation

Keep a copy of your insurance policy, emergency assistance number and key coverage limits:
- In your phone (offline accessible — screenshot the key pages)
- In your luggage (physical copy)
- With a family member or contact at home

### Pre-Existing Conditions

If you have any medical condition, declare it honestly during purchase. Most insurers offer coverage for pre-existing conditions at higher premiums; a policy that covers your specific condition is worth the extra cost. An undisclosed pre-existing condition claim may be denied entirely.

### Claims Documentation

If you receive medical treatment in China:
- Keep all receipts (收据) — insist on them if not offered
- Get a translated medical report if possible (international hospitals do this routinely)
- Photograph prescription bottles and medication receipts
- Note the name and address of each medical facility
- Keep any diagnostic test results

### Mobile Phone Coverage

Increasingly, travel insurance policies cover phone theft; the coverage is typically ¥1,000–¥2,000 ($140–$280). Compared to the actual replacement cost of a modern smartphone, this is insufficient. Consider a separate device insurance policy or ensure your home contents insurance covers devices internationally.

## Coverage for Specific China Activities

### Trekking and Hiking

- Most standard policies cover day hiking
- Multi-day trekking above 4,000m: Requires either specialist policy or specific high-altitude add-on
- Technical climbing (ropes, anchors): Requires adventure policy
- Guided trekking below 3,000m: Usually covered by standard policy

### Cycling

- Road cycling: Usually covered
- Mountain biking: Often excluded or requires add-on
- E-bikes: Check specifically; policies vary

### River Rafting

- Commercially organised white water rafting: Requires adventure add-on on most policies
- Grade I–II (gentle water) commercial tours: Often covered by standard

### Winter Sports/Skiing

- Coverage for skiing at established Chinese resorts (Zhangjiakou, Harbin, Urumqi): Available as winter sports add-on (typically +$20–$50 for 2 weeks)

## Key Exclusions to Be Aware Of

**Non-emergency treatment:** Insurance covers emergency medical care, not elective procedures, dental checkups or scheduled treatments.

**Alcohol-related incidents:** Many policies exclude claims arising from incidents where you were legally intoxicated. China has zero-tolerance drink-driving (relevant if renting a vehicle or motorbike).

**Civil unrest:** Most policies exclude events arising from civil unrest or political instability; standard travel doesn't typically encounter this risk in China.

**Travelling against FCO/Government advice:** If your government has issued advice against travel to a specific area of China and you go anyway, claims arising in that area may not be covered.

## What to Do in a Medical Emergency

1. **Call your insurer's emergency assistance line** — immediately or as soon as you're able
2. **Go to the nearest appropriate hospital** (for serious emergencies, don't wait for insurer approval)
3. **Call your country's embassy or consulate** if you need help with language, doctors or family contact
4. **For non-emergency medical needs:** Major city hospitals with international departments in Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Chengdu and other large cities have English-speaking staff

## Final Word

Travel insurance for China should be purchased before departure, not as an afterthought. The investment — typically $80–$200 for a 2-week trip — is trivial compared to the financial exposure of an uninsured medical emergency.

Spend 30 minutes comparing policies, check the activity exclusions, ensure altitude coverage if applicable, save the emergency number, and then travel without anxiety. The insurance exists precisely so you don't have to worry about it until you need it.
