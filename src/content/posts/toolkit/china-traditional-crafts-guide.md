## Table of contents

China's craft traditions represent one of the most extraordinary repositories of human skill and cultural knowledge on earth. Some techniques — such as silk production and porcelain glazing — were kept secret for centuries, triggering international espionage and shaping global trade routes. Others, like shadow puppetry and paper-cutting, are folk traditions practiced in rural farmhouses for thousands of years.

For travellers, these crafts offer a rare window into how Chinese culture actually transmits itself across generations — not through textbooks but through hands and patience.

---

## The Great Chinese Craft Traditions

### Silk (丝绸 — Sīchóu)

Silk production in China dates back at least 5,000 years. China held a monopoly on silk production for much of ancient history, and the Silk Road trade routes were named for this one commodity.

**Where to see it**: The best silk regions are Suzhou (known for intricate brocade weaving), Hangzhou (for silk fabric production), and Guangzhou (for Cantonese embroidery). The **China Silk Museum** (中国丝绸博物馆) in Hangzhou is the definitive resource — free entry, exceptional collections, and a working weaving demonstration section. Open daily, 9am–5pm.

**Where to buy quality silk**: 
- Suzhou's Silk Museum and affiliated shop: ¥180–880 ($25–123) for scarves; ¥580–3,500 ($81–490) for larger pieces
- Hangzhou's Silk Market (丝绸市场): More commercial, negotiation expected. Budget ¥100–500 ($14–70) for scarves; ¥300–2,000 ($42–280) for fabric by the meter.

**Learning to embroider**: Suzhou embroidery (苏绣) workshops accept short-term students. A 2–3 hour taster class costs ¥180–380 ($25–53). Longer residential courses are available for serious learners. The **Suzhou Embroidery Research Institute** (苏州刺绣研究所) is the most prestigious organisation.

**Distinguishing real silk**: Burn test — genuine silk goes out immediately and smells like burned hair; synthetics continue to burn and smell like plastic. A simple test vendors won't mind if you ask for a loose thread.

### Porcelain and Ceramics (瓷器 — Cíqì)

Jingdezhen is the porcelain capital of the world, which is a title it has legitimately held for over 1,700 years. The name "china" (lowercase) for ceramics came directly from this city's historical dominance.

**Jingdezhen** (景德镇), Jiangxi Province: The entire city revolves around ceramics — from industrial factories producing export ware to tiny studios where individual artists hand-throw pieces in thousand-year-old styles. Must-visit spots:

- **Imperial Kiln Site Museum** (御窑博物馆): Built over archaeological excavations, with stunning modern architecture encasing ancient kiln ruins. Admission ¥60 ($8.40). Hours: 9am–5pm, closed Mondays.
- **Taoxichuan Creative District** (陶溪川): Former state ceramic factory converted to an arts district. Weekend evening markets, galleries, and workshops. Many studios accept walk-in visitors.
- **Sanbao Village** (三宝): Pottery village 7km from the city, home to serious ceramic artists and the Sanbao Ceramics Cultural Village. Stay here for a true immersive experience. Studios offer throwing classes from ¥150–350 ($21–49).

**Other ceramics destinations**:
- **Dehua** (德化), Fujian: White porcelain (白瓷) capital. The creamy white figures are extraordinary.
- **Shiwan** (石湾), Foshan, Guangdong: Rough stoneware tradition with distinctive glazes and earthy aesthetic.
- **Yixing** (宜兴), Jiangsu: Unglazed purple clay (紫砂) teaware. Yixing teapots are among China's most collected ceramic forms; prices range from ¥50 ($7) for factory pieces to ¥50,000+ ($7,000+) for master craftwork.

### Lacquerware (漆器 — Qīqì)

Lacquer crafts in China date back over 7,000 years. The process involves applying dozens of layers of resin from the lacquer tree (漆树) to carved wood, bamboo, or fabric bases. The result is extraordinarily durable and can achieve vivid reds, blacks, and greens.

**Best places to find quality lacquerware**:
- **Fuzhou**, Fujian: Famous for "bodiless lacquerware" (脱胎漆器) — pieces where the lacquer layers are built up around a clay mould, which is then removed. The result is incredibly light but strong.
- **Chengdu**, Sichuan: Carved lacquerware with deep relief designs
- **Pingyao** and **Shanxi province**: Lacquerware with local motifs

Prices range from ¥50 ($7) for small decorative pieces to ¥3,000–20,000 ($420–2,800) for museum-quality works.

### Paper-Cutting (剪纸 — Jiǎnzhǐ)

Chinese paper-cutting is a UNESCO-listed intangible cultural heritage. The art involves cutting intricate patterns from single sheets of red or coloured paper using scissors or craft knives. Traditional motifs include flowers, fish, animals of the zodiac, and theatrical characters.

**Regional styles**:
- **Shaanxi** (particularly Ansai County): Bold, strong designs; considered the most ancient tradition
- **Hebei** (particularly Yuxian): Vivid multi-coloured paper-cutting using dyed papers
- **Shanxi**: Delicate, intricate window designs (窗花)
- **Fujian and Guangdong**: More curvilinear, influenced by tropical motifs

**Where to learn**: 
Paper-cutting classes are widely offered in tourist areas. Look for workshops in:
- Xi'an's Shuyuanmen Cultural Street: ¥60–100 ($8–14) for a 1-hour class
- Beijing's hutong cultural centres: ¥80–150 ($11–21)
- Shanghai Old City area: ¥80–120 ($11–17)

A simple paper-cutting takes 20–30 minutes to learn basics; master-level pieces take years. Even a beginner's attempt makes an excellent personalised souvenir.

### Wood Block Printing and Woodcarving (木版画 / 木雕)

**New Year prints (年画 — Nián Huà)**: Traditional woodblock-printed New Year pictures, once produced in every rural household to decorate homes during Spring Festival. Major production centres:

- **Yangliuqing** (杨柳青), near Tianjin: Most famous New Year print tradition; the printing workshop is open to visitors. Admission ¥30 ($4.20); print-your-own sessions available for ¥50–80 ($7–11).
- **Taohuawu**, Suzhou: Suzhou-style New Year prints, more delicate in colour palette.
- **Weifang**, Shandong: Known also for kite art alongside woodblock prints.

**Woodcarving (木雕)**: Dongyang, Zhejiang is China's woodcarving capital. The city produces elaborate architectural carvings and standalone art pieces. Visiting the woodcarving market area in Dongyang reveals the full scope of the craft — from small decorative panels for ¥50 ($7) to room-sized architectural installations costing hundreds of thousands.

### Shadow Puppetry (皮影戏 — Pí Yǐng Xì)

One of China's oldest performing arts forms, with a 2,000-year history. Shadow puppets are intricately carved from donkey or ox hide, then painted and assembled with articulated joints. Performances involve the puppets being held against a lit screen, creating a theatrical shadow play.

**Where to see shadow puppetry**:
- **Xi'an** and **Guanzhong region**, Shaanxi: The heartland of Chinese shadow puppet tradition. The Tang Dynasty Art Museum and various hutong theatres offer performances.
- **Tangshan**, Hebei: Northern shadow puppet tradition
- **Chengdu**: Sichuan opera theatres sometimes include shadow puppet segments

**Buying puppets**: Tourist areas sell both handmade puppets (¥80–500/$11–70) and factory copies (¥20–60/$3–8). The quality difference is visible — handmade pieces have more refined cutting and more intricate paintwork.

### Batik (蜡染 — Là Rǎn) and Tie-Dye (扎染 — Zhā Rǎn)

Both textile traditions are particularly strong in ethnic minority regions:

**Batik**: Guizhou and Yunnan, practiced primarily by Miao (苗族) and Buyi (布依族) minority women. Hot wax is applied to fabric, which is then dyed — the wax resists the dye to create patterns. Beautiful blues and whites result.

**Tie-dye (Zhā Rǎn)**: Most famous in **Dali**, Yunnan, practiced by Bai minority artisans. The Zhoucheng village 15km north of Dali Old Town is the tie-dye production centre. Workshops offer visitor participation for ¥50–100 ($7–14). Finished scarves sell for ¥40–120 ($5.60–17).

### Cloisonné (景泰蓝 — Jǐngtài Lán)

This metalworking tradition involves creating decorative objects by filling enamel paste between metal wire outlines applied to bronze or copper surfaces. The brilliant colours — named for a Ming Dynasty blue glaze (景泰蓝 literally means "Jingtai blue") — became synonymous with high court taste.

**Beijing** is the centre of cloisonné production. The **Beijing Cloisonné Factory** (北京珐琅厂) in Chongwen offers tours of the production process. Quality ranges from decorative tourist pieces (¥50–200/$7–28) to genuine artisan works (¥2,000–50,000/$280–7,000+).

## How to Identify Authentic Handmade Crafts vs Mass Production

This is the central challenge for craft buyers in China. Mass-produced items dominate tourist shops, often priced only marginally below handmade equivalents.

**General indicators of handmade quality**:
- Minor irregularities and asymmetries in ceramics and weaving
- Slightly uneven glaze distribution
- Tool marks visible under magnification
- Production certificates or artist signatures
- Watching the item being made (the gold standard)

**Red flags for mass production**:
- Perfectly identical items in a large stack
- Prices dramatically below market value for the craft type
- Vendor unable to explain the production process
- "Factory tour" that turns out to be a showroom

**Best practice**: Buy directly from artisan workshops and studios rather than tourist gift shops. The price difference is often small, and you gain confidence in authenticity.

## Craft Learning Centres and Schools

For serious learners who want more than a one-hour taster class:

**Jingdezhen Sanbao International Ceramics Art Institute**: Week-long to month-long residential programs for ceramics. Accommodation available on site. Fees vary significantly — email directly for current pricing.

**Suzhou Embroidery Research Institute**: Short courses for visiting artists and crafts students. The institute occasionally accepts foreign participants.

**Guizhou Ethnic Minority Cultural Centres**: Several NGO-supported centres in Guizhou's Miao villages offer multi-day batik and embroidery workshops. Search for "Miao textile workshop Guizhou" for current programs.

**National Art Museum courses**: Major art museums in Beijing and Shanghai occasionally run traditional craft workshops for adult learners. Check their programs in Chinese on their official websites.

## Shipping Crafts Home

Chinese crafts make excellent gifts and souvenirs, but some items require care in transport:

- **Ceramics**: Use abundant bubble wrap and mark "FRAGILE" clearly. Professional packing services are available in Jingdezhen and major craft centres for ¥30–80 ($4–11).
- **Lacquerware**: Transport at ambient temperature — extreme cold can cause cracking.
- **Silk**: Fold rather than roll for long storage; keep away from moisture.
- **Cloisonné**: Usually quite robust; standard packing is adequate.

International shipping from China: EMS (China Post Express Mail) is reliable and tracks well. DHL, FedEx, and UPS offices exist in major cities. Budget ¥150–500 ($21–70) for small-medium packages depending on weight and destination.

China's craft traditions are most rewarding when experienced as living practices rather than museum pieces. Seek out the workshops, watch the hands at work, and — wherever possible — try the craft yourself. The resulting purchase, even if imperfect, carries something a tourist-shop piece never can: the memory of making it.
