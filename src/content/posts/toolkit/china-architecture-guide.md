## Table of contents

China's built environment tells the story of one of history's most complex civilisations, overlaid with the disruptions of colonialism, revolution, rapid industrialisation, and an extraordinary construction boom that has changed the skyline of virtually every Chinese city within a single generation.

For architecture enthusiasts, few countries offer comparable richness. The challenge is knowing what to look for and where.

## Traditional Chinese Architecture

### The Principles

Traditional Chinese architecture is governed by principles very different from Western building traditions:

- **Timber-frame construction**: Chinese buildings are traditionally constructed with a timber skeleton that supports the roof; the walls between are non-load-bearing. This is opposite to Western masonry construction.
- **Horizontal rather than vertical**: Chinese architecture values breadth and courtyard complexity over height. Palaces spread across hundreds of hectares; church steeples are not part of the aesthetic.
- **Roof as focal point**: The curved, sweeping roof with upturned eaves (飞檐) is the most visually distinctive element of Chinese traditional architecture. The curve is functional (throwing rainwater away from walls) as well as symbolic.
- **Cosmological alignment**: Major complexes are oriented on a north-south axis. The most important structures face south toward yang energy; the north is defensive.
- **Courtyard organisation**: Traditional Chinese living units and institutional complexes are organised around courtyards (院落, yuànluò). The Forbidden City is essentially a series of nested courtyards.

### Major Architectural Categories

**Palace Architecture (宫殿建筑)**: The most elaborate expression of traditional Chinese building. Characterised by yellow glazed roof tiles (symbolising imperial power), red painted pillars, white marble terraces, and precise spatial hierarchy.

**Best examples**:
- **Forbidden City (故宫)**, Beijing: The world's largest palace complex. 180 acres, 9,000+ rooms, 600 years of history. Admission ¥60 ($8.40); timed tickets required. Essential.
- **Palace Museum's Wenhua Hall and Wuying Hall**: Lesser-visited sections of the Forbidden City with exceptional interiors.
- **Shenyang Imperial Palace (盛京皇宫)**: The Qing dynasty's ancestral palace before Beijing conquest. Smaller but extraordinary; less crowded. Admission ¥60 ($8.40).

**Temple Architecture (寺庙建筑)**: Buddhist, Taoist, and Confucian temples follow similar principles to palace architecture but adapted for religious use. Gate guardians, incense courtyards, main halls, and secondary halls are arranged in formal procession.

**Best examples**:
- **Yonghe Temple (雍和宫)**, Beijing: Active Tibetan Buddhist temple in Beijing; extraordinary interior with 18m Maitreya Buddha. Free admission.
- **Confucius Temple (孔庙)**, Qufu: The largest Confucian temple complex, in Confucius's home town in Shandong. ¥90 ($12.60).
- **Lingyin Temple (灵隐寺)**, Hangzhou: One of China's most important Chan Buddhist temples, in a forest setting. ¥45 ($6.30).

**Vernacular Architecture (民居)**: Regional folk architecture is extraordinarily diverse:
- **Huizhou style (徽派建筑)**: White-plastered walls, dark grey tile roofs, horse-head gables. Centered in Anhui (Hongcun, Xidi) and Jiangxi (Wuyuan). UNESCO-listed villages.
- **Fujian Tulou (福建土楼)**: Circular or square communal earthen fortresses built by Hakka and Minnan clans. UNESCO-listed; most accessible from Xiamen.
- **Siheyuan courtyard houses (四合院)**: Beijing's traditional courtyard houses, diminishing rapidly but surviving in hutong areas. Best preserved examples around Shichahai and Dongcheng District.
- **Diaojiaolou (吊脚楼)**: Stilted wooden houses of the Tujia, Miao, and Dong ethnic minorities in Hunan, Guizhou, and Chongqing. Visually dramatic, often built on steep hillsides.

## Colonial Architecture

China's treaty port history left an extraordinary variety of European, American, and Japanese architectural styles embedded in coastal cities.

### Shanghai: The Colonial City Par Excellence

Shanghai was carved into foreign concessions from 1842, and the buildings that resulted are some of the finest examples of colonial-era architecture in Asia.

**The Bund (外滩)**: The famous mile-long waterfront boulevard lined with 52 major buildings in Neoclassical, Art Deco, Renaissance Revival, and other European styles. The Hong Kong and Shanghai Banking Corporation building, the Customs House, the Peace Hotel — each is a distinct architectural statement. The Bund is free to walk; several buildings are open to the public.

**French Concession (法租界)**: Tree-lined streets, Art Deco apartment buildings, lanes (弄堂) of attached townhouses. The architectural texture here is extraordinarily preserved compared to much of central Shanghai. Wander the lanes around Wukang Road, Hunan Road, and Xingguo Road.

**Jewish Heritage Architecture**: The Hongkew area (now Hongkou) preserves architecture from the Jewish refugee community of World War II. The Ohel Moshe Synagogue is now a Jewish Refugees Museum. Free.

### Tianjin: Five Countries, One City

Tianjin was divided into more foreign concessions than any other Chinese city — British, French, American, German, Japanese, Italian, Russian, Belgian, and Austrian concessions all left distinct architectural legacies.

**Italian Concession (意大利风情区)**: The most intact of Tianjin's foreign concession areas. Neo-Renaissance buildings along streets named for Italian explorers. Free to wander; some buildings open as restaurants and galleries.

**Five Avenues (五大道)**: The former British Concession's residential area, with large European-style villas. Beautiful tree-lined streets. The best architectural walking district in Tianjin.

**Tianjin Attractions fee**: Most concession areas are free to walk; specific museum buildings charge ¥20–50 ($2.80–7).

### Qingdao: Germanic China

Germany controlled Qingdao from 1898–1914, and the legacy is a remarkably intact German colonial town — red-tiled roofs, pointed gables, stone churches, and cobblestone streets.

**Old Town (老城区)**: The German quarter around Zhongshan Road and Badaguan area. Walking the streets feels genuinely European. The Qingdao Cathedral (¥10/$1.40 entry) is the most iconic German-era building.

**Badaguan scenic area**: A collection of different national architectural styles in villas built during various colonial periods. Some buildings are open; most can be admired from outside.

### Kulangsu Island (鼓浪屿), Xiamen: UNESCO International Settlement

Designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2017 for its "historic International Settlement" architecture. The island (car-free) is covered with colonial-era villas in British, American, Dutch, Spanish, and Portuguese styles, alongside traditional Minnan (Southern Fujian) architecture. Entry to the island: ¥35–55 ($4.90–7.70) including ferry.

## Silk Road and Western Architecture

Northwestern China preserves architectural traditions fundamentally different from both Han Chinese and colonial European styles.

### Xinjiang: Central Asian and Islamic Architecture

The great mosque of **Kashgar's Id Kah** is the most significant Islamic architectural monument in China — a grand mosque with a yellow facade and twin minarets serving the Uyghur community. The old city around it, though significantly rebuilt in recent years, retains Central Asian character.

**Turpan**: Ancient ruins of Jiaohe (交河故城) and Gaochang (高昌故城) — pre-Islamic Silk Road cities built from compressed earth. The ruins are extraordinary and unlike anything else in China. Jiaohe: ¥75 ($10.50).

### Gansu: Mud Brick and Caravanserai

**Jiayuguan Fort (嘉峪关城楼)**: The western terminus of the Ming Great Wall. The fort rises dramatically from the edge of the Gobi Desert. ¥120 ($16.80) for the fort complex.

**Zhangye Danxia**: Not architecture per se, but the multi-coloured rock formations have been compared to an architectural wonder of nature. ¥75 ($10.50).

## Soviet-Influenced Architecture

China's Soviet era (1950–1960s friendship period) left significant architectural deposits, particularly in northeastern China.

**Harbin (哈尔滨)**: Built initially by Russians around the Trans-Siberian Railway, then further influenced by Soviet modernism. The city's central Stalin Street (中央大街, now a pedestrian zone) is lined with Russian-style buildings. The iconic Saint Sophia Cathedral (¥15/$2.10) is a Russian Orthodox masterpiece. Harbin is unlike any other Chinese city architecturally.

**Shenyang**: Soviet-era industrial architecture alongside earlier Japanese colonial buildings and the original Manchu imperial palace. The city's variety of 20th-century styles is remarkable.

## Contemporary Chinese Architecture

China has become the world's most active arena for contemporary architecture. The scale of construction since 1990 means that virtually every major architect on earth has built significant projects here.

### Beijing: Olympic Legacy and More

**National Centre for the Performing Arts (国家大剧院)** by Paul Andreu: The "Giant Egg" — a titanium and glass ellipsoid floating in an artificial lake. One of the world's most dramatic concert halls. Tours available.

**CCTV Headquarters (央视总部大楼)** by Rem Koolhaas/OMA: The loop-shaped skyscraper that challenged every assumption about what a tower could be. Best viewed from 3rd Ring Road East.

**National Stadium (鸟巢/Bird's Nest)** by Herzog & de Meuron: The Olympic stadium, its tangled steel exterior remains one of architecture's most recognisable forms. Admission to walk inside: ¥50 ($7).

**National Aquatics Centre (水立方/Water Cube)**: The ETFE plastic bubble structure adjacent to the Bird's Nest. Now functions as a water park. Exterior viewing free.

### Shanghai: Pudong's Skyline Competition

The Pudong skyline is the world's most dramatic collection of signature towers — the Oriental Pearl Tower (1994), Jin Mao Tower (1999), Shanghai World Financial Center (2008, the "bottle opener"), and Shanghai Tower (2015, 632m, China's tallest building).

**Shanghai Tower** by Gensler: The twisted, double-curtain-wall tower is an engineering landmark. Observation deck on the 118th floor: ¥180 ($25.20). The highest outdoor observation deck in China.

**The Bund area contemporary additions**: Several significant contemporary buildings now bookend the historical Bund, including the HSBC Building renovation and various gallery conversions.

### Shenzhen: China's Urban Experiment

Shenzhen, which essentially didn't exist as a city before 1980, is China's most fascinating case study in rapid urban development.

**OCT Loft (华侨城创意文化园)**: A converted industrial complex now housing galleries, design studios, and cafes. One of China's best examples of adaptive reuse architecture.

**Ping An Finance Centre**: At 599m, this tower by Kohn Pedersen Fox is currently the 4th tallest building in the world. Observation deck: ¥220 ($30.80).

## Architecture Tourism Tips

**Walking tour resources**: Several cities have self-guided architectural walking tours. Notably:
- Shanghai French Concession: Multiple guidebooks and WeChat apps cover this area
- Beijing hutong areas: Various hutong tour organisations (Walking Great Wall, various hutong agencies)

**Opening hours**: Temples and palaces typically open 8am–5pm (shorter in winter). Colonial-era streets are accessible year-round. Contemporary observation decks operate 9am–10pm at most towers.

**Photography**: Most architectural exteriors can be photographed freely. Some interiors (Forbidden City main halls, religious interiors) prohibit photography or flash photography.

**Architectural appreciation tip**: For any traditional Chinese complex, walk through sequentially from south to north, entering through the main southern gate. The spatial experience of Chinese architecture is sequential — understanding this transforms how you experience the Forbidden City, any temple, or any formal garden.

---

China's architectural spectrum — from 3,000-year-old rammed earth cities to 600-metre glass towers, from Silk Road mud fortresses to Art Deco jazz club facades — is arguably the broadest of any country in the world. A trip focused specifically on architecture never runs out of material.
