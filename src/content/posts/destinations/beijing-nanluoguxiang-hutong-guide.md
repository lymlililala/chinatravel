The hutongs are Beijing's soul. The grids of grey-walled alleyways that spread across the old city contain courtyard homes (*siheyuan*), family-run noodle shops, boutique coffee roasters, tiny theatres, and an entire way of life that has survived — in compressed and tourist-adjacent form — the relentless demolitions of the 20th century.

Nanluoguxiang (南锣鼓巷) is the most famous hutong street and, consequently, one of Beijing's most crowded. But immediately around it, and in the several other hutong zones scattered across the second ring road, you can walk for hours through genuinely quiet old Beijing. This guide covers both — the iconic and the hidden.

## Understanding the Hutong

**What is a hutong?** The word comes from Mongolian — it means a narrow lane or passage. The hutong system was Beijing's urban fabric for 700 years, a grid of east-west alleys connecting north-facing courtyard homes. The homes themselves (四合院, *siheyuan*) are built on a traditional plan: a central courtyard surrounded by single-storey rooms on all four sides, with the main building facing south to capture winter sun.

At their peak, Beijing had over 7,000 hutongs. By 2010, fewer than 1,000 remained. The surviving hutongs are now protected, but many have been gentrified or commercialised. The challenge for visitors is finding the balance between atmosphere and authenticity.

## Nanluoguxiang: What to Expect

Nanluoguxiang itself is an 800-metre north-south street — technically not a hutong (which are east-west) but a perpendicular connecting street. It became the epicentre of Beijing's indie café and boutique culture around 2010, then became so popular it turned primarily commercial.

**Be honest about expectations:** On a weekend afternoon in summer, Nanluoguxiang is a shoulder-to-shoulder tourist crowd eating ice cream and buying screen-printed T-shirts. It's still worth visiting — some genuinely good food and shops remain — but it requires managing your timing.

**Best time:** Weekday mornings (before 10 AM) or winter evenings.

### What to Eat on Nanluoguxiang
- **炒肝 (Stir-fried liver)**: Thick sauce of pig liver, intestines, and starch — a specific Beijing breakfast dish. **Yaoji** (姚记炒肝) at the north end of the street has been making this since 1979.
- **冰糖葫芦 (Candied hawthorn skewers)**: The most photogenic Beijing street snack — shiny red globes on a bamboo stick. Available from carts year-round, cheaper in winter.
- **褡裢火烧 (Belt-clasp flatbread)**: Shallow-fried dough pockets stuffed with minced pork and chives. Several small stalls sell these; look for the ones with a queue.
- **酸梅汤 (Sour plum drink)**: A sweet-tart cold drink made from dried plum, hawthorn, osmanthus flowers, and licorice. The classic Beijing summer drink; far better than the bottled version.

### The Side Hutongs Off Nanluoguxiang

The best exploration starts when you turn off the main street. From Nanluoguxiang, four east-west hutongs in particular are worth exploring:

- **Mao'er Hutong (帽儿胡同):** Contains several former Beijing mansions (王府, *wangfu*) — official residences of Qing dynasty princes. The street itself is beautifully preserved with high grey walls and carved gate piers.
- **Ju'er Hutong (菊儿胡同):** Features the famous experimental courtyard architecture project by architect Wu Liangyong — modern housing that reinterprets the traditional siheyuan. Unusual and worth seeing.
- **Banchang Hutong (板厂胡同):** Residential and quiet, with a cluster of independent cafés favoured by local creative workers.
- **Dongmianhua Hutong (东棉花胡同):** Home to the **Central Academy of Drama** and several independent theatre spaces. Students and rehearsal noise spill onto the street at all hours.

## Wudaoying Hutong (五道营胡同)

One east-west alley running parallel to the outer wall of the Lama Temple (*Yonghegong*) — and far more pleasant than Nanluoguxiang at almost any hour.

Wudaoying became Beijing's best café street through a combination of low rents (for years) and its proximity to the internationally-attended Chinese college sports scene. The result: a 600-metre stretch of independent coffee shops, small wine bars, natural wine shops, vinyl record stores, and bookshops, with almost no large-chain intervention.

**Highlights:**
- **Soloist Coffee (独奏者):** Single-origin pour-overs; serious about sourcing
- **Slow Boat Taproom:** Beijing's most consistent craft brewery, with a tap room on the hutong
- Several Tibetan-influenced design shops selling thangka-style artwork and handmade jewellery, spillover from the nearby Lama Temple

**Combine with:** The **Lama Temple (雍和宫)** at the east end of the hutong is Beijing's largest and most important Tibetan Buddhist temple — free with ID, impressive architecture and atmosphere, open daily 9 AM–4 PM.

## Fangjia Hutong (方家胡同)

Between the Lama Temple and the Drum Tower, Fangjia is less known than both Nanluoguxiang and Wudaoying but has a strong local creative community. The former site of an old factory has been converted into a small arts district with theatre spaces, design studios, and a cluster of bars that run late.

**MAO Livehouse Beijing** was here — now relocated, but the area maintains its live music and underground arts reputation. Good for an evening drink.

## The Drum Tower Area: Shichahai

The most atmospheric hutong neighbourhood overall centres on the **Drum Tower (鼓楼)** and **Bell Tower (钟楼)** — two enormous medieval tower-and-drum complexes that told time for the entire city for five centuries — and the **Shichahai** lakes to their south.

The three interconnected lakes (Qianhai, Houhai, and Xihai) are surrounded by willows, ancient courtyard homes, and the former mansions of Qing Dynasty princes. The area is slightly more upmarket than the Drum Tower hutongs to the north, with lakeside bars and restaurants.

**What to do here:**
- Climb the **Drum Tower** (¥30) for a view across the grey-tile roofscape of the old city — arguably the most authentic view of "old Beijing" available
- Walk north from the Drum Tower into **Jiudaowan Hutong (九道湾胡同)** — a winding alley that turns nine times (hence the name), deep residential, no shops
- Hire a bicycle from the Shichahai area and explore north toward the Second Ring Road — the hutong grid is navigable and most streets connect
- Late evening: the lakeside area around **Houhai** (后海) has a ring of bars popular with young locals, especially in summer when outdoor seating extends over the water

### Prince Gong's Mansion (恭王府)

On the west side of Houhai: the best-preserved example of a Beijing *wangfu* (prince's mansion), covering nearly 6 hectares with garden, opera stage, and residential halls. Tickets ¥40; guided tours include a traditional Peking opera performance.

## Getting Around the Hutongs

**Walking** is the fundamental mode. The alleys are too narrow for cars in most places, and the experience is tactile — the smell of coal smoke and cooking, the sound of conversations behind high walls, the cats sleeping in doorways.

**Cycling** extends your range considerably. Meituan Bike (美团单车) and Hello Bike (哈啰单车) dockless bikes are available throughout the hutong areas and can be unlocked with Alipay or WeChat Pay at ¥1.5/30 minutes. This is exactly how locals navigate between hutong zones.

**Rickshaw tours** (人力三轮车) are aggressively marketed near Nanluoguxiang and at the Drum Tower. They are expensive (¥100–200/person for a tour) and the commentary varies wildly. If you want a guided introduction, they're functional — negotiate the price, duration, and route explicitly before getting in.

## Practical Tips

**Do not use Google Maps inside China** (it doesn't work without a VPN). Download **Baidu Maps** or **Amap (高德地图)** for navigation. Both have English interfaces and pedestrian routing through hutongs.

**Hutong etiquette:** The alleys pass in front of private homes. Residents do live here. Photographing people in their doorways or peering over walls is not appreciated; asking permission first is.

**Refreshments:** There are very few public toilets in hutong areas beyond designated public facilities at major entry points. Locate these before you go deep.

**Seasonal note:** Hutong walks are best in spring (April–May, blooming locust trees) and autumn (September–October, cooler temperatures). Summer afternoons are brutal. Winter is cold but has a particular atmosphere — coal smoke rising from courtyards, bare trees against grey sky, locals wrapped in heavy coats cycling past.
