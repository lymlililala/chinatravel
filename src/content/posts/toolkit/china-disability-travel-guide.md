Travelling China with a mobility disability or wheelchair is genuinely possible, but it requires more preparation than in many Western countries, and the accessibility information available online is often optimistic rather than accurate. This guide aims to give honest, practical information about what works and what doesn't.

The good news: China's modern infrastructure — new metro systems, G-trains, international hotels — is designed to modern accessibility standards and works well. The challenge: China's most historically and culturally significant sites are ancient structures built without accessibility in mind, and retrofitting them ranges from incomplete to impossible.

## High-Speed Train Accessibility

China's high-speed G and C trains are among the most accessible in Asia:

**Wheelchair spaces:** Every G-train has dedicated wheelchair spaces in carriages 5 and 16 (on standard configurations). These spaces are wider than standard seats and positioned near the accessible toilet. To use them, book the wheelchair space specifically when purchasing tickets — they're not automatically allocated with a standard purchase.

**Booking wheelchair spaces:** On Trip.com or 12306.cn, look for the disability assistance booking option. Alternatively, booking at the train station counter directly allows you to request the wheelchair space. Bring documentation if you have it (a disability certificate or letter from a doctor), though this isn't always required.

**Accessible toilets:** G-trains have spacious accessible toilets in the dedicated wheelchair carriage. They are regularly cleaned on longer routes.

**Platform access:** Major stations (Beijing South, Shanghai Hongqiao, Guangzhou South) have excellent lift access, accessible gates, and staff assistance services. Smaller stations are more variable. Call ahead to the station to request platform assistance — the number is on the station website or through China Rail Customer Service.

**What doesn't work:** The older K and T trains (conventional speed) have minimal accessibility provisions. Hard sleeper carriages are completely inaccessible for wheelchair users. If you use a wheelchair, use G or D trains for inter-city travel.

## Metro Accessibility

**Shanghai:** One of the world's most accessible metro systems. Nearly all stations on Lines 1-13 have lifts. The accessibility lifts are clearly signed with blue signage and are consistently maintained. Station staff are trained to assist disabled passengers.

**Beijing:** Good accessibility on most lines, with lifts at over 90% of stations on the main lines. The older Line 1 and Line 2 stations are the least accessible; newer lines (6, 14, 15, 16) are fully accessible.

**Guangzhou:** The newer sections (Lines 2, 3, 7, 8) are fully accessible. Some original Line 1 stations have limited lift access.

**Chengdu and other major cities:** Generally good on new lines, variable on older infrastructure.

**The app for metro access:** Download the city metro app (Shanghai Metro, Beijing Subway, etc.) — most show which stations have lifts and which exits have step-free access.

## Major Tourist Sites: Honest Assessment

### Accessible (with some limitations)
- **National Museum of China (Beijing):** Wide galleries, lift access throughout, accessible toilets. One of China's most wheelchair-accessible museums.
- **The Forbidden City (Beijing):** The main north-south axis is paved and relatively level. The side courtyards and secondary palaces involve some steps and uneven stone surfaces. A wheelchair can navigate the central route with assistance. The Treasure Gallery involves a step at the entrance — staff will assist.
- **Temple of Heaven (Beijing):** The park itself is largely accessible. The circular temple structures involve steps but can be viewed from ground level.
- **West Lake (Hangzhou):** The lakeside paths are paved and flat. Cycling the lake circuit on an electric wheelchair or accessible bike hire is genuinely enjoyable.
- **Giant Buddha at Leshan:** The river boat view of the Buddha from the water is fully accessible. The cliff-side steps to the Buddha's feet are not (170 narrow steps cut into the rockface — not manageable in a wheelchair).

### Genuinely Challenging
- **The Great Wall:** Every section involves significant steps. Mutianyu has a cable car for the ascent but the wall itself is stone steps with no ramp option. The wall cannot be meaningfully visited by wheelchair from the top.
- **Huangshan (Yellow Mountain):** The trails are stone steps. Cable cars are accessible but the summit paths involve uneven stone surfaces.
- **Zhangjiajie:** The National Forest Park has a mix of accessible boardwalks and inaccessible stone paths. The most famous viewpoints (Yuanjiajie) require some navigation of uneven terrain.
- **Tiger Leaping Gorge:** Not accessible by wheelchair. The high trail is a mountain footpath with steep sections.
- **Old town streets in Lijiang, Pingyao:** Stone cobblestones and raised doorway thresholds throughout. Manageable with powered wheelchairs and determination; challenging in manual chairs.

## Accessible Hotels

International hotel chains (Marriott, Hilton, Intercontinental, AccorHotels) at their 4-5 star properties in major Chinese cities maintain international accessibility standards: roll-in showers, wider doorways, lowered bathroom facilities, and accessible room layouts.

**Booking tip:** When booking, specify your requirements explicitly in Chinese (轮椅无障碍客房 = wheelchair accessible room) as well as English. Call the hotel directly to confirm the room layout before your visit — hotel websites sometimes describe non-accessible rooms as "accessible" because they have a grab bar.

**Chinese domestic hotels:** Accessibility standards are more variable. Budget hotels and local guesthouses rarely have roll-in showers or accessible bathrooms, though they may have ground-floor rooms. Check carefully before booking.

## Requesting Assistance

**The magic phrase:** "请问有无障碍设施吗？" (Qǐngwèn yǒu wúzhàng'ài shèshī ma?) — "Excuse me, is there disabled access?" Having this on your phone to show is practical.

**Airport assistance:** China's major airports provide wheelchair assistance services. Book in advance through your airline or at the assistance desk in the departure terminal.

**Train station assistance:** Call ahead (China Rail customer service) or arrive early to request platform assistance. Staff at major stations are generally willing to help but may not approach proactively.

## Finding Reliable Accessibility Information

The challenge in China is that official tourism materials routinely describe sites as "accessible" when the actual situation involves steps, uneven surfaces, or incomplete accessibility provision. Strategies for finding accurate information:

1. **Contact the site directly** (or have someone contact on your behalf in Chinese) to ask specific questions: "Does the entry have ramps or steps? Is there lift access to all floors?"

2. **Look for recent visitor reports** from disabled travellers — the Disabled Travelers Facebook group and similar communities have China-specific discussions.

3. **Contact a specialist agency:** Accessible China Travel (accessiblechina.org) and similar operators have ground-truthed accessibility information and can arrange appropriate tours.

4. **Use the Google/Baidu Street View equivalent (Baidu Maps' street-level panoramas)** to visually check the entrance and approach to specific sites.

The honest bottom line: major modern infrastructure in China's tier-1 cities is genuinely accessible and improving. Ancient sites, natural scenic areas, and older smaller cities present real challenges. Plan specifically for each destination rather than assuming a general level of accessibility.
