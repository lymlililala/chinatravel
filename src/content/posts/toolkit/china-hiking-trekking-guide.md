China has some of the most dramatic hiking terrain on earth — from the subtropically green gorges of Yunnan to the limestone spires of Zhangjiajie, the sacred peaks of Huangshan and Taishan, the wild unrestored sections of the Great Wall, and the high-altitude trekking circuits of Sichuan and Tibet. For hikers, China offers more variety within a single country than almost anywhere else in the world.

This guide covers the best hiking and trekking experiences for foreign visitors — from single-day accessible walks to multi-day wilderness treks — with difficulty ratings, logistics, and what to prepare.

## The Sacred Mountains (五岳 — Five Great Mountains)

China's Five Sacred Mountains are the most historically significant mountain hikes in the country. Pilgrims have climbed them for over 2,000 years; emperors performed heaven-earth ceremonies at their summits. Today they are a mix of ancient temple architecture, spectacular scenery, and sometimes overwhelming tourist crowds.

### Taishan (泰山) — Most Sacred, Most Crowded

**Location:** Tai'an, Shandong
**Height:** 1,545 metres
**Hike duration:** 4–7 hours ascent on foot (2 hours descent); cable car option available
**Difficulty:** Moderate — 7,000 steps but well-maintained

Taishan is the most visited sacred mountain in China, with over 6 million visitors per year. The traditional route ascends via the Tianzhu Peak Path (天烛峰景区 route less crowded) or the Central Route through Dai Temple. The summit at night, when pilgrims camp to see the sunrise (日出) over the Yellow River plain, is an experience unlike any other in China.

**Best time:** October–November (clear air, fewer visitors than summer). Avoid National Day week.

### Huashan (华山) — Most Dangerous, Most Thrilling

**Location:** Huayin, Shaanxi (near Xi'an)
**Height:** 2,154 metres
**Hike duration:** 5–8 hours for full circuit (North/South/East/West/Middle peaks)
**Difficulty:** Strenuous to extreme — chains bolted into vertical rock faces, narrow cliff-side plank walks, and near-vertical stairs

Huashan has a justified reputation as one of the world's most dramatic mountain hike experiences. The "Plank Walk in the Sky" (长空栈道) section involves traversing a wooden plank ledge on a cliff face with only chains to hold — hundreds of metres above the valley. You strap into a harness but the path is narrow and the exposure is real.

This is a mountain where the experience of the ascent itself is the primary attraction. The views from the summit peaks are extraordinary.

**Night hiking:** Many visitors take the cable car up late at night (midnight), hike to the East Peak to watch sunrise, then descend on foot. The night-time scenery and dawn light on the granite peaks make this the recommended approach.

**Safety note:** Huashan's exposed trails demand appropriate footwear (hiking shoes minimum), a head torch for night ascent, and basic fitness. The harness sections are technically safe for most people but not recommended for anyone with fear of heights.

## Tiger Leaping Gorge, Yunnan — The Classic Trek

**Location:** 100km north of Lijiang, Yunnan
**Duration:** 2-day trek (1 overnight stay at guesthouse)
**Difficulty:** Moderate with some strenuous sections
**Distance:** ~29km from Qiaotou to Daju (or reverse)

Tiger Leaping Gorge is one of Asia's most famous trekking routes: a 16km gorge through which the Jinsha River (upper Yangtze) flows between Jade Dragon Snow Mountain (5,596m) and Haba Snow Mountain (5,396m), with the gorge floor 3,000 metres below the summit ridges.

### The Upper Trail (Recommended for Trekkers)

The two-day upper trail on the gorge's northern rim is the classic route:

**Day 1 (Qiaotou to Halfway Guesthouse or Sean's Spring):** 8–10 hours, with significant elevation gain. The "28 Bends" — 28 switchback turns on the initial steep ascent — is the most demanding section (1.5–2 hours). Once above the bends, the trail traverses high above the gorge with views of both snow mountains.

**Day 2 (Halfway to Tina's/Walnut Grove then Daju):** 5–7 hours. The trail descends through terraced fields and villages into the lower gorge area.

**Guesthouses:** Several family-run guesthouses along the route (Halfway Guesthouse, Sean's Spring, Tea Horse Guesthouse) provide beds, basic meals, and shower facilities. Book ahead during busy seasons (May, October). Cost: ¥60–150 per person per night.

**What to bring:**
- Hiking boots (essential — the trail has loose gravel and some scrambling)
- 2L+ water capacity
- Trekking poles (optional but helpful on descents)
- Rain gear (afternoon storms possible in summer)
- Headtorch
- Cash (no ATMs on the trail — bring ¥500+ per person)

## Jiankou Great Wall — Wild Section for Serious Hikers

**Location:** 90km northeast of Beijing, Huairou District
**Duration:** Full day (6–8 hours)
**Difficulty:** Strenuous — unrestored Wall requires scrambling, fallen towers, gaps in the Wall
**Special character:** The most photogenic section of the Great Wall, appearing in almost every aerial Great Wall photograph

Jiankou is an unrestored Great Wall section: no handrails, no restored steps, no cable car, and no tourist facilities. It offers the Wall as it has looked for the past 400 years — crumbling, overgrown with trees, dramatically wild.

**Standard route:** Jiankou to Mutianyu (approximately 8km along the Wall ridgeline, finishing at the restored and cable-car-served Mutianyu section). Take a private car to Jiankou village base, hike up to the Wall, traverse eastward to Mutianyu, descend by cable car, arrange pickup at Mutianyu entrance.

**Why it's extraordinary:** The Jiankou section's steep ridgeline position (the Wall literally perches on a knife-edge ridge) means the views are more dramatic than any restored section. The Beijing knot (北京结) — where the Wall meets from multiple directions — is one of the great Great Wall views.

**Safety:** Proper hiking boots essential. Sections with steep scrambles require hands and feet. Not appropriate for casual tourists. Do not attempt in ice or wet conditions.

## Huangshan (Yellow Mountain) — Sunrise Trek

**Location:** Southern Anhui Province
**Height:** 1,864m (Lotus Peak, highest point)
**Duration:** 2-day minimum (overnight on the summit to see sunrise)
**Difficulty:** Moderate — cable cars available to the summit area; significant stair climbing required for the peak circuit

Huangshan is China's most aesthetically significant mountain — the inspiration for traditional Chinese landscape paintings (山水画), with its granite peaks emerging above a sea of clouds, ancient pine trees growing horizontally from cliff faces, and strange rock formations.

**The experience:** Huangshan is designed for the two-day/one-night approach. Take the western cable car up on Day 1 afternoon. Hike the peak circuit (West Sea Grand Canyon and Northern route) to one of several summit hotels. Wake at 5am to watch sunrise from Bright Summit Peak (光明顶) or Begin-to-Believe Peak (始信峰) — the cloud sea below and the first light illuminating the granite is the defining Chinese landscape experience.

**Practical notes:**
- Summit hotels are expensive (¥500–1,200 per night for a basic twin room) but the overnight experience is worth the cost
- Book summit hotels well in advance, especially for autumn (October–November)
- The sea of clouds is not guaranteed — it requires specific weather conditions; spring and autumn have the highest probability

## Zhangjiajie National Forest Park — Avatar Mountains

**Location:** Western Hunan Province
**Duration:** 2–3 day exploration
**Difficulty:** Easy to moderate — extensive cable car, elevator, and path infrastructure

Zhangjiajie's "pillar mountains" — sandstone columns rising hundreds of metres vertically from the valley — inspired the floating mountains of the Avatar (2009) film and have since become one of China's most photographed landscapes.

**The hiking routes:**
- **Tianmen Mountain Glass Bridge** — a glass-bottomed suspension bridge 300m above the valley floor
- **Yuan Family Community elevated pathway** — a cliff-side footpath at 2,000m elevation with views over the pillar forest
- **Tianzi Mountain Nature Reserve** — cable car to ridgeline with multiple path options through the pillars
- **Golden Whip Brook (金鞭溪)** — an 8km forest valley walk at the base of the pillars: clear stream, monkeys, no elevation change

## Sichuan High Altitude Trekking

### Siguniang Mountain (四姑娘山)

**Location:** 220km northwest of Chengdu, Aba Prefecture
**Height:** Three treks: Shuangqiao Valley (accessible day hike), Changping Valley (1-day moderate), Haizi Valley (multi-day, permit required above base)
**Difficulty:** Moderate to strenuous depending on route

Siguniang Mountain (Four Sisters Mountain) is a cluster of four peaks, the highest reaching 6,250m. The Shuangqiao Valley is accessible without special permits and provides some of the most spectacular sub-alpine scenery in China — glacial peaks, wildflower meadows, yak herds, and Tibetan villages.

**When to go:** June–October. The Shuangqiao Valley wildflower peak is late June–July.

### Daocheng-Yading (稻城亚丁)

**Location:** Ganzi Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, extreme western Sichuan
**Height:** 3,700–4,700m (main circuit)
**Difficulty:** Moderate to strenuous at altitude

Yading Nature Reserve contains three snow peaks (Chenresig at 6,032m, Chenadorje at 5,958m, and Jampayang at 5,958m) and the Milk Lake (牛奶海) and Five Colour Lake, both above 4,500m. The main trekking circuit passes all three peaks over two days.

**Altitude note:** Acclimatise at 3,700m (Daocheng town level) for at least one night before attempting higher circuits.

## Preparation Essentials for Hiking in China

**Gear:**
- Hiking boots (not trainers) for any mountain hike
- Rain gear (Chinese mountain weather can change rapidly)
- Layer system (temperatures at altitude are significantly lower than valley)
- Trekking poles for steep descents
- Head torch and spare batteries
- Emergency whistle

**Health:**
- Altitude sickness medication (acetazolamide / Diamox) for anything above 3,000m — consult a doctor before departure
- Personal first aid kit
- Travel insurance including mountain rescue and helicopter evacuation

**Logistics:**
- Permits required for some areas (Tibet requires a Tibet Travel Permit; certain Sichuan nature reserves require tickets)
- Guide services recommended for non-trail routes
- Tell someone your route and expected return time for multi-day treks
