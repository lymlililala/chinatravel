## The Reality of Remote Work in China

![Jinshanling Great Wall, Beijing](/img/toolkit/china-travel-freelancer-guide/1.webp)

Let us start with the uncomfortable truth: China does not have a digital nomad visa, and freelancing on a tourist visa is technically illegal. Thousands of remote workers do it anyway, collecting salaries from overseas clients while hopping between Chinese cities on L or M visas. The risk is real but manageable if you understand the landscape. This guide gives you the honest picture — what the rules say, what people actually do, and how to navigate the gap between the two.

Why bother at all? Because China is an extraordinary place to work remotely from. The cost of living is a fraction of Western levels. The food is incredible. The high-speed rail network lets you work from a different city every week. And the sheer energy of the place — the construction cranes, the new subway lines, the relentless pace of change — creates a creative buzz that is hard to find elsewhere.

## Visa Options for Remote Workers

### The L Visa Approach (Tourist)

Most remote workers enter China on an L (tourist) visa, typically a 10-year multiple-entry visa for US, Canadian, UK, Australian, and many EU citizens. Each entry allows 60–90 days, after which you must leave and re-enter.

**Pros:** Easy to obtain, no Chinese employer involvement, minimal documentation.

**Cons:** You cannot legally work — not for a Chinese company and not remotely for an overseas one. Immigration authorities have become more aware of "visa runners" and may question frequent re-entries.

**Staying compliant-ish:**
- Keep a low profile. Do not advertise that you are working.
- Do not use a Chinese address for business registration or tax purposes.
- Maintain a paper trail showing you are a tourist (hotel bookings, tourist site tickets, travel photos).
- Exit and re-enter through different ports when possible.
- Consider the 144-hour visa-free transit option for shorter stays.

### The M Visa Approach (Business)

The M (business) visa is slightly more appropriate for remote workers who have legitimate business connections in China — attending trade shows, meeting suppliers, conducting market research. Some freelancers obtain M visas through Chinese business contacts who provide invitation letters.

**Pros:** More legitimate cover for extended stays. Multiple-entry options. Less scrutiny than L visa runners.

**Cons:** Still does not permit actual work. Requires a genuine Chinese business connection. Your inviter becomes responsible for your activities in China.

### The X1 Visa Approach (Student)

Enrolling in a university language program is the most legitimate pathway for a long stay. X1 student visas convert to residence permits valid for the duration of your study (typically 6–12 months for language programs). You can work part-time legally (up to 20 hours per week with permission).

**Tuition costs:** University language programs range from ¥10,000–30,000 per year. Private language schools charge ¥8,000–25,000 for 6-month programs.

**Pros:** Fully legal long stay. Residence permit. Part-time work permission. University resources (libraries, gyms, cafeterias).

**Cons:** You actually have to attend classes. Minimum attendance requirements exist. Costs more than a tourist visa.

### The Z Visa Approach (Employment)

If you can secure a job with a Chinese company, the Z visa gives you a residence permit and full legal status. Some remote workers find part-time teaching or consulting positions that provide Z visa sponsorship while they continue their primary freelance work.

**Warning:** Your Z visa is tied to your sponsoring employer. Moonlighting on a Z visa is illegal. If you are caught working a second job, you risk deportation.

## Internet Access and VPN Solutions

### The Great Firewall Reality

China's internet censorship blocks Google services (Gmail, Google Drive, Google Maps), all Meta platforms (Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp), most Western news sites, and numerous other services that remote workers depend on. Without a VPN, you cannot access these tools.

### VPN Recommendations for 2026

The VPN landscape in China changes constantly. Services that work perfectly one month may be disrupted the next. As of early 2026, these options have the best track records:

- **Astrill VPN:** The most reliable for China, with dedicated stealth servers. ¥60–90/month. Expensive but worth it for work-critical connectivity.
- **ExpressVPN:** Generally reliable with fast speeds. ¥80–100/month. Occasional disruptions during politically sensitive dates.
- **NordVPN:** Budget-friendly at ¥35–50/month with obfuscated servers. Speeds can be inconsistent.
- **Shadowrocket / Clash:** Protocol-based solutions popular among tech-savvy expats. Require a server subscription (¥20–60/month) plus the app purchase. Most flexible but needs technical setup.

**Critical advice:** Download and install your VPN **before** entering China. The VPN provider websites are blocked inside the country. Configure auto-connect and backup servers.

### Mobile Internet

Chinese 4G/5G networks are fast, extensive, and affordable:

- **China Unicom SIM:** Best for foreigners. ¥100/month for 20GB data. Available at airport kiosks with your passport.
- **China Mobile SIM:** Largest network coverage, especially in rural areas. ¥80/month for 15GB.  
- **Virtual eSIM:** Services like Airalo and Holafly offer eSIM plans that work alongside your home SIM. ¥40–80 for 5–10GB. Convenient but more expensive per GB than local SIMs.

**Pro tip:** Get both a local SIM (for Chinese apps and domestic calls) and keep your home SIM (for receiving verification codes from overseas services). A dual-SIM phone is essential.

## Co-Working Spaces by City

### Shanghai

- **WeWork (多址):** 8+ locations across Shanghai. Day passes from ¥120. Monthly desks from ¥2,000. Reliable high-speed internet, free coffee, and community events. The WeWork at Shanghai Tower (Lujiazui) has spectacular views.
- **Naked Hub:** Chinese co-working brand with stylish spaces. ¥100–150/day pass. Locations in Jing'an, Xuhui, and Pudong.
- **Impact Hub Shanghai:** Social enterprise focused co-working. ¥80/day pass. Smaller but more community-oriented.

### Beijing

- **WeWork:** 6+ locations. Day passes from ¥120. The Guomao and Sanlitun locations are popular with international remote workers.
- **Upermitted Space:** A boutique co-working space in Sanlitun. ¥100/day. Great coffee and a curated community of creatives and entrepreneurs.
- **Daydayup:** Chinese co-working with locations in CBD and Zhongguancun. ¥80–120/day. Strong tech community.

### Shenzhen

- **WeWork:** 4 locations. ¥100–120/day. The Nanshan location is convenient for tech workers.
- **TechSpace Shenzhen:** In Nanshan's tech district. ¥60–100/day. Popular with hardware entrepreneurs and software developers.
- **SZOIL (Shenzhen Open Innovation Lab):** Free access to the maker space. ¥50/day for desk space. Ideal if you are working on hardware projects.

### Chengdu

- **DreamPlus:** Local chain with 3 locations. ¥60–80/day. The Chengdu IFS location is central and well-equipped.
- **WeWork:** 2 locations. ¥100/day. The Chunxi Road space is steps from the panda-shaped IFS tower.
- **Cohub:** A smaller, community-focused space in Wuhou District. ¥50/day. Excellent for networking with Chengdu's growing startup scene.

### Hangzhou

- **WeWork:** 3 locations. ¥80–100/day. The Huanglong万科 location is convenient for Alibaba-adjacent professionals.
- **Lionspace:** Local co-working with locations near West Lake. ¥60/day. More laid-back atmosphere.

## Banking and Getting Paid

![Yiyuanjie Scenery, Beijing, Suzhou](/img/toolkit/china-travel-freelancer-guide/2.webp)

### Receiving Money as a Remote Worker

This is one of the biggest headaches. Chinese banks are restrictive about foreign currency inflows, and WeChat/Alipay are not designed for receiving international payments.

- **Wise (formerly TransferWise):** Receive money to a Wise account in your home currency, then transfer to a Chinese bank account in RMB. Fees are 0.5–2% depending on the currency pair. Most reliable option for regular transfers.
- **PayPal:** Works in China but with high fees (2.9% + ¥0.30 per transaction) and unfavourable exchange rates. Use as a backup.
- **Bank wire transfer:** Direct international wire to your Chinese bank account. ¥150–200 in receiving fees from the Chinese bank, plus your sender's wire fee. Processing takes 3–5 business days. You will need to visit the bank in person with your passport to set up foreign currency receiving.

### Opening a Chinese Bank Account

You need a residence permit (from an X1, Z, Q1, or S1 visa) to open a bank account in most cases. Bank of China, ICBC, and China Merchants Bank are the most foreigner-friendly. Bring your passport, residence permit, and a Chinese phone number. The process takes about 1 hour.

Without a residence permit, some branches of Bank of China will open basic savings accounts for L visa holders, but policies vary by branch and change frequently. Try branches in expat-heavy areas (Shanghai's Jing'an, Beijing's Chaoyang) where staff are more accustomed to foreign customers.

## Tax Obligations

### Do You Owe Chinese Tax?

If you are physically present in China for 183 days or more in a calendar year, you are generally considered a tax resident and owe Chinese income tax on worldwide income. The rates are progressive, from 3% (under ¥36,000/year) to 45% (over ¥960,000/year).

If you are present for fewer than 183 days, you only owe Chinese tax on income derived from Chinese sources. Income from overseas clients, received in overseas bank accounts, while you are in China on a tourist visa, occupies a grey area. The Chinese tax authorities have limited ability to track this income, but the legal obligation technically exists.

**Practical advice:** Consult a tax professional in both your home country and China. Double taxation agreements exist between China and most Western countries and can prevent you from being taxed twice on the same income.

### The 6-Year Rule

For foreign nationals without a domicile in China, income from overseas sources paid outside China is exempt from Chinese individual income tax for up to 6 consecutive years of residency. If you leave China for more than 30 consecutive days in any year, the clock resets. This is relevant for Z visa holders planning extended stays.

## Daily Life Logistics

### Accommodation

- **Short-term apartments:** Airbnb and Chinese platforms like Ziroom (自如) and Heyzhiju (合住集) offer furnished apartments from ¥2,000–5,000/month in Tier 1 cities. Ziroom requires a residence permit; Airbnb does not.
- **Serviced apartments:** ¥5,000–15,000/month. Includes cleaning, gym, and front desk. Oakwood, Ascott, and Somerset are reliable international chains.
- **Shared housing:** ¥1,500–3,500/month for a private room in a shared apartment. Listings on SmartShanghai (Shanghai) and The Beijinger (Beijing) classifieds.

### Health Insurance

China's public healthcare is inexpensive (a hospital visit costs ¥50–200) but crowded and challenging to navigate without Mandarin. International clinics in major cities charge ¥500–1,500 per visit but offer English-speaking doctors and Western standards.

Consider travel health insurance or international health plans. Cigna Global, Allianz, and Now Health International offer plans specifically designed for expats and remote workers in China. Budget ¥3,000–10,000/year depending on coverage level.

### Staying Productive

- **Time zone management:** China is UTC+8 with no daylight saving time. If your clients are in Europe, you are 6–8 hours ahead. If they are on the US East Coast, you are 12–13 hours ahead. Many remote workers in China adopt a split schedule — Chinese mornings free for exploration, afternoons and evenings for work aligned with Western business hours.
- **Coffee shop working:** Starbucks Reserve Roasteries (Shanghai, Beijing) and independent speciality cafés (Seesaw, Manner, % Arabica) offer free Wi-Fi and are generally tolerant of laptop campers. Order something every 2–3 hours.
- **Noise levels:** Chinese cafés can be loud. Invest in noise-cancelling headphones. The Bose QuietComfort or Sony WH-1000XM5 are worth the ¥2,500–3,000 investment.

## Pros and Cons Summary

| Advantage | Disadvantage |
|-----------|-------------|
| Low cost of living | Internet restrictions require VPN |
| World-class infrastructure | No digital nomad visa |
| Incredible food everywhere | Language barrier outside major cities |
| Fast, cheap domestic travel | Banking complications for foreigners |
| Safe cities, low street crime | Air pollution in northern cities |
| Vibrant, energetic culture | Visa stress and uncertainty |

Working remotely from China is not the easiest digital nomad setup in Asia — Thailand, Vietnam, and Bali are simpler. But for those willing to navigate the complexities, China offers depth, diversity, and a creative intensity that few other countries can match. Come with patience, a reliable VPN, and a sense of humour, and you may find it becomes your favourite place to work from.
