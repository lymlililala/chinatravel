Bamboo does something odd to your sense of distance. On the plateau above Changning, in the far south of Sichuan, the moso stems rise forty or fifty feet before they branch, and the light that reaches the ground has been filtered through so many layers of leaf that it arrives green. Walk a hundred metres in and the road behind you has gone. Stand still and the whole forest moves at once in a long dry rustle that sounds unnervingly like surf — which is where the name comes from. Shunan Bamboo Sea (蜀南竹海) is not a grove. It is a landscape made of one plant.

## What This Place Actually Is

![Tianfuguosheyingyi, Sichuan, Chengdu](/img/destinations/shunan-bamboo-sea-sichuan-guide/1.webp)

Shunan sits on the boundary between Changning and Jiang'an counties, on high ground reached by a long climb from the Yibin plain. The reserve covers a rumpled plateau of low summits, side valleys and stream beds, and almost all of it is under bamboo — dozens of species, though the moso that dominates is the one that gives the forest its cathedral quality. Villages and small farms are folded into it. So are temples, a Qing-era cliff fortress, a scatter of lakes and half a dozen waterfalls.

What makes it worth the detour is not any single sight. It is the sheer monotony of the material, and the way that monotony becomes hypnotic rather than dull. There is no view from a ridge here that resolves into "mountains" or "villages" — you look out over a green swell that runs to the horizon and breaks in the wind. Parts of Ang Lee's *Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon* were shot in this forest, and the association is still visible in the way visitors behave: people stop mid-path, look up, and go quiet.

The other reason to come is the food, which is unusually specific to the place. More on that below.

## The Lie of the Land

Two facts about the terrain will save you time. First, the plateau is red sandstone — the soft, rust-coloured rock that produces Danxia landforms elsewhere in southern China. It shows up in cliff faces, in the paving of the main forest road, and in the way streams have cut narrow slots rather than broad valleys. The contrast between that dull red stone and the bamboo above it is the visual signature of Shunan, and it is strongest on a wet day.

Second, the reserve is long and awkward. It stretches roughly east to west, with the main gate near the small town of Wanling on the western side and a second cluster of sights around the cliff temples in the middle. The distances between named attractions are real distances — several kilometres in places, up and down. Nearly everyone uses a mix of shuttle buses, the cable car, and short stretches on foot, and that is the right approach.

## The Sights Worth Your Legs

### The Emerald Corridor (翡翠长廊)

If you see one thing, see this. A stretch of forest road runs for a couple of kilometres between banks of mature moso that lean inward until they almost meet overhead. The surface is that red sandstone, so from a distance you are looking down a green tunnel with a rust-coloured floor. The road rises and dips, which keeps the tunnel from feeling like a set piece, and there is enough traffic-free stretch to walk it properly.

Go early. By late morning in high season this is the most photographed spot in the reserve and the corridor fills with tripods. In light rain it is better than in sun — the sandstone darkens to something closer to brick, and mist collects between the stems.

### Xianyu Cave (仙寓洞) and the cliff carvings

On the southern side of the plateau, a natural overhang in the sandstone has been built out into a shallow cliff temple. A single narrow path gets you in, which was the point — the site was defensible. Inside, Ming-dynasty relief carving covers the rock: a reclining Buddha cut directly into the red stone, ranks of arhats, a Guanyin hall where water drips through the ceiling into a basin. Some of the carving is crude and some of it is very good indeed, and the whole thing has the damp, moss-edged texture of a place that has never been fully dry.

Allow an hour. It is dark, so give your eyes time.

### Tianbao Fortress (天宝寨)

A short distance from the cave, a Qing-era defensive gallery runs along the inside of a cliff for well over a kilometre. Local officials cut it into a line of natural half-caves to hold off an advancing army, and the result is a corridor of stone gates and switchbacks with bamboo pressing in at the open side. You are walking along a cliff face the whole way, screened by the forest, which takes most of the vertigo out of it. Look for the enormous mushroom-shaped boulder on the route — a wide stone cap on a narrow stem, apparently on the point of toppling for the last few centuries.

This is the best hour of walking in the reserve, and it works in any weather.

### The waterfalls and the lakes

Streams here run off the plateau in steps. The largest fall drops in a series of tiers into a gourd-shaped valley between two hills, with a viewing platform below and a small pavilion above; in high water the noise fills the whole valley, and in the dry months it thins to a set of separate ribbons. There are more falls than the map suggests, and the smaller unnamed ones on the walk down are often better than the headline act.

The lakes are quieter propositions — flat green water ringed by bamboo, with rafts poled out onto them. A raft trip is short and unspectacular and I would do it anyway, because being down at water level with bamboo on all sides is the only way to see the forest reflected properly. Fairy Lake, on high ground above the cliff temples, gets the best morning light.

### The bamboo museum

Skip it if you are pressed. Do not skip it if you have any interest in craft. The collection covers the region's bamboo trades — weaving fine enough to reproduce classical paintings in split fibre, carving in bamboo root and in the thin inner skin, furniture, containers. Some of the woven pictures are astonishing objects, made from filaments split thinner than thread, and the museum explains what you are looking at outside better than any signboard on the trails does. Read more about regional craft traditions in our [culture](/tags/culture) coverage.

## Walking, Riding and the Cable Car

The cable car is worth using in at least one direction. It climbs from a valley floor up to the ridge near the observation tower and the Emerald Corridor, and the ride is long — long enough that the novelty of skimming over the tops of bamboo wears off and you start noticing the structure of the forest, the paler new growth, the gaps where a stand has been cut for timber. Ride it up and walk down if you have the knees for it.

Shuttle buses run between the main clusters of sights inside the reserve. Build your day around where they stop rather than around a straight line on the map. Hiring a car with a driver makes sense for three or four people who want to cover both ends without waiting about; ask at your guesthouse rather than at the gate.

For anyone who wants to walk properly, the linking paths between Wangyou Valley, the stream courses and the museum are shaded and largely level, and you will have long stretches to yourself. If you enjoy that sort of thing, our [hiking](/tags/hiking) archive has more of it.

## Wangyou Valley (忘忧谷) After Dark

![Tianfuguosheyinger, Sichuan, Chengdu](/img/destinations/shunan-bamboo-sea-sichuan-guide/2.webp)

The name translates roughly as the Valley of Forgotten Sorrows, which usually signals a disappointment. In this case the valley delivers, mostly because of when you go. A stream runs down it between rock walls, with a paved path alongside. Come at dusk or after, when the day visitors have gone, and the acoustics take over: water on stone, the click of stems knocking together, occasional frogs. On a clear night with a moon up you can walk it without a torch, and the light coming through bamboo is genuinely strange — dappled, moving, colourless. Staying inside the reserve is what buys you this.

## Getting There and Away

Yibin is the gateway. The city sits at the confluence of two rivers at the head of the Yangtze proper, it has an airport with domestic connections, and it is on the high-speed rail network, which has made the approach from Chengdu and Chongqing much less of an ordeal. From Yibin, frequent coaches run out to Changning county town, and from Changning local buses and shared cars climb the last stretch to the reserve gates.

Direct coaches from Chengdu run through to the bamboo sea, which saves changing but takes most of the day. Drivers come down the expressway to Yibin and then up the hill; allow the best part of a morning, and expect the final climb to be winding.

Yibin also sits close to Li Zhuang, a riverside town of old timber halls and famously good thin-sliced pork, so the two make an obvious pair on the way back. See our wider [Sichuan](/tags/sichuan) itineraries for how it fits into a longer loop.

## Seasons

**Spring** brings new shoots up in their thousands, pushing through the leaf litter beside the paths in a matter of days. The forest looks scruffier than in photographs and smells strongly green. Rain is frequent.

**Summer** is the local reason for coming. The plateau stays genuinely cool while the basin below is unbearable, and Chinese families arrive in numbers for exactly that. Waterfalls are at their fullest. Book well ahead.

**Autumn** is the best all-round choice: stable weather, low humidity, the bamboo still fully green, and scattered red leaves from broadleaf trees mixed into the groves. Crowds thin after the early-October holiday week.

**Winter** brings cold, still, empty days and occasional snow, which loads the stems until they bend into arches. It is the most photogenic version of the place and the least comfortable. Nights are cold; the day-to-night swing up here is larger than visitors expect in any season, so pack a warm layer even in July.

## Where to Stay

![Lugu Lake Ligeban Island Scenery, Sichuan](/img/destinations/shunan-bamboo-sea-sichuan-guide/3.webp)

Staying inside the reserve is the right call, and there are three broad options. Farm guesthouses in the hamlets near the museum and the cable car are the most local — plain rooms, good home cooking, and hosts who will tell you which stream is running well. Mid-range hotels cluster along the main road through the plateau and near the temple sights, the pragmatic choice for families who want reliable hot water. At the top end, a large resort on the outskirts trades on hot springs and a lake; it is comfortable and somewhat detached from the forest.

Changning county town and Yibin city are fallbacks if the plateau is full, but you lose the evenings, which are the point.

## What to Eat

Shunan has a genuinely distinctive local table, and the headline is the all-bamboo banquet — a sequence of dishes in which nearly every component comes from the forest. Done well it is not a gimmick: shoots sliced thin and stir-fried clean with almost nothing added, so they taste of themselves; steamed dishes served in bamboo tubes; soups clouded with bamboo fungus.

Two things to look for specifically. The first is the lacy-collared bamboo fungus that grows in the litter here, usually simmered slowly with a free-range chicken into a broth that smells of the forest floor in the best possible way. The second is the immature form of the same fungus, sold as bamboo eggs — pale, egg-shaped, crisp, coated in a slick jelly, and genuinely difficult to find anywhere else.

Shoots are available all year because different species come into season in rotation, so there is no wrong month for them. Beyond bamboo, this is Yibin country, which means the region's famous dry-tossed noodles — no broth at all, just oil, chilli, crushed peanut and sesame, tossed hard — and leaf-wrapped glutinous rice parcels sold at festivals. In the old town below, a cool rice jelly is made with water from a spring that bubbles continuously. For more on the region's cooking, see our [food](/tags/food) guides.

## Honest Practicalities

**What to skip.** The reconstructed pavilions and minor named viewpoints scattered along the roads add very little; there are dozens of them and they blur. Skip the smaller lakes if you have already been on a raft. Skip the souvenir arcades near the gates entirely — the same bamboo trinkets appear everywhere, and the good craft is in the museum.

**Do not plan by attraction list.** The reserve publishes a long inventory of scenic points and it is a trap. Pick three or four anchors, use the shuttles, and leave time to simply be in the forest between them.

**Weather changes the experience more than the season does.** Fog and drizzle are common and they improve almost everything here. Bring a light waterproof rather than an umbrella; the paths are narrow.

**Footwear matters.** Sandstone steps grow moss and stay damp for days after rain. Carry water for the longer walks, too — refreshment stops cluster near the gates and thin out badly in between.

Two nights and a full day and a half, with early starts, and you will leave with the sound of it still in your head.
